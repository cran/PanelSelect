1. Introduction

PanelSelect provides a unified and flexible framework for sample selection models with continuous, binary, and count outcomes in panel and cross-sectional data. In particular, it supports a broad family of panel selection models, which extend the classic Heckman framework to account for persistent unobserved heterogeneity. The package supports selection at two levels: the individual level, through correlated random effects, and the observation level, through correlated occasion-specific shocks. This allows researchers to distinguish non-random selection driven by persistent individual characteristics from selection driven by temporary shocks. Alongside model estimation, PanelSelect supports prediction of population-level outcomes.

Each selection model combines a probit selection equation with an equation for a continuous, binary, or count outcome, estimated jointly by maximum likelihood. For panel data, both equations include individual random effects to capture persistent unobserved heterogeneity. The corresponding likelihoods are detailed in Bailey and Peng (2025) and Peng and Van den Bulte (2024). PanelSelect evaluates the required integrals using fixed Gauss–Hermite quadrature (the default), adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature, or Gauss–Legendre quadrature, which can improve accuracy in difficult cases but may require substantially more computation.

For data with one observation per individual, the package also supports cross-sectional linear, binary, and Poisson-lognormal selection models. The remainder of this vignette focuses on the panel models, demonstrating how to specify and fit them and assess numerical accuracy.

1.2. What changed since 1.0.1?

Version 2.0.0 is a full refactor of the package, developed with assistance from Codex and Claude Code. We recommend 2.0.0 for new analyses and for refitting analyses conducted with 1.0.1. The four original panel-selection families are retained, with improvements that matter for everyday use:

  • Corrected estimation. Corrections to likelihood and score calculations address some errors that can slightly distort parameter estimates and standard errors.
  • A unified, user-friendly interface. panel_select() replaces separate model-specific fitting functions, with common controls and standard methods such as summary(), predict() and update().
  • Broader model support. Cross-sectional selection models and standalone binary and count panel models complement the original panel-selection families.
  • More control over numerical accuracy. Adaptive Gauss–Hermite (AGH) and Gauss–Legendre (GL) supplement fixed Gauss–Hermite (GH). Together with explicit numerical checks, they help detect and address inaccurate integration in difficult cases.
  • Faster fitting in general. The numerical engine has been optimized for better performance.

The migration vignette gives the main changes needed to update existing code.

2. Supported Panel Selection Models

The panel selection models share a probit selection equation and differ in their outcome specifications. We begin with the notation and assumptions used throughout this section.

For individual \(i\) on occasion \(t\), let \(d_{it}\) indicate participation and let \(y^*_{it}\) denote the potential outcome, observed only when \(d_{it}=1\). The row vectors \(\mathbf{w}_{it}\) and \(\mathbf{x}_{it}\) contain the selection and outcome covariates, with coefficient vectors \(\boldsymbol{\alpha}\) and \(\boldsymbol{\beta}\), respectively. Persistent individual effects are denoted by \(u_i\) and \(v_i\), while \(\varepsilon_{it}\) and \(\epsilon_{it}\) denote occasion-specific shocks.

Individuals are assumed to be independent, and individual effects are independent of the covariates. Occasion-specific shocks are independent across occasions and of the individual effects, while selection and outcome shocks on the same occasion may be correlated. Further model details appear in Bailey and Peng (2025) and Peng and Van den Bulte (2024).

2.1. Continuous Outcome (Probit RE–Linear RE)

The selection and outcome equations are

\[ d_{it}=1\bigl(\mathbf{w}_{it}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\delta u_i+\varepsilon_{it}>0\bigr), \]

\[ y^*_{it}=\mathbf{x}_{it}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\lambda v_i+\sigma\epsilon_{it}. \]

The correlation structure is

\[ \begin{pmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal{N}_2\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{it}\\\epsilon_{it}\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal{N}_2\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1&\tau\\\tau&1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

Use this model for continuous outcomes with conditionally normal errors. The scales \(\delta\), \(\lambda\) and \(\sigma\) are the SDs of selection effects, outcome effects and outcome errors. The correlations \(\rho\) and \(\tau\) capture persistent and same-occasion dependence. The selection shock has variance one.

2.2. Binary Outcome (Probit RE–Probit RE)

For a binary potential outcome,

\[ d_{it}=1\bigl(\mathbf{w}_{it}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\delta u_i+\varepsilon_{it}>0\bigr), \]

\[ y^*_{it}=1\bigl(\mathbf{x}_{it}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\lambda v_i+\epsilon_{it}>0\bigr), \]

with

\[ \begin{pmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal{N}_2\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{it}\\\epsilon_{it}\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal{N}_2\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1&\tau\\\tau&1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

Use this model for binary outcomes. Both occasion-shock variances are normalized to one.

2.3. Poisson Count Outcome (Probit RE–Poisson RE)

For count outcomes with selection only through individual effects,

\[ d_{it}=1\bigl(\mathbf{w}_{it}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\delta u_i+\varepsilon_{it}>0\bigr), \]

\[ y^*_{it}\mid\mathbf{x}_{it},v_i \sim\operatorname{Poisson}\!\left\{ \exp(\mathbf{x}_{it}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\lambda v_i)\right\}, \]

where

\[ \begin{pmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal{N}_2\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

\[ \varepsilon_{it}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1). \]

The outcome random effect allows for overdispersion arising from unobserved individual heterogeneity. Conditional on the individual random effects, selection and the Poisson outcome are independent, so the model has no separate occasion-specific correlation parameter \(\tau\).

2.4. Poisson-Lognormal Count Outcome (Probit RE–PLN RE)

The Poisson-lognormal model extends the Poisson specification by adding an observation-level random effect to the log rate. This effect can be correlated with the selection shock \(\varepsilon_{it}\), allowing for same-occasion selection dependence. The equations are:

\[ d_{it}=1\bigl(\mathbf{w}_{it}\boldsymbol{\alpha}+\delta u_i+\varepsilon_{it}>0\bigr), \]

\[ y^*_{it}\mid\mathbf{x}_{it},v_i,\epsilon_{it} \sim\operatorname{Poisson}\!\left\{ \exp(\mathbf{x}_{it}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\lambda v_i+\gamma\epsilon_{it})\right\}, \]

with

\[ \begin{pmatrix}u_i\\v_i\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal{N}_2\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1&\rho\\\rho&1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

\[ \begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon_{it}\\\epsilon_{it}\end{pmatrix} \sim\mathcal{N}_2\!\left( \begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix}1&\tau\\\tau&1\end{pmatrix}\right). \]

Here \(\gamma\) measures the standard deviation of the additional random effect at the observation level. PLN accommodates overdispersion beyond that captured by individual effects and allows selection and outcome shocks to be correlated within occasions.

3. Simulated Example: Estimation and Numerical Checks

3.1. Simulate a panel with known parameters

The example follows the Linear specification. Both equations contain the same two covariates, with coefficients one, and all three SDs equal one. The correlations are \(\rho=-0.5\) and \(\tau=0.5\):

\[ d_{it}=1\bigl(x_{it}+w_{it}+u_i+\varepsilon_{it}>0\bigr), \]

\[ y^*_{it}=x_{it}+w_{it}+v_i+\epsilon_{it}. \]

We simulate 500 people over five occasions. Both covariates are independent standard normal variables, independent of the latent disturbances. The code retains unselected rows and records their outcomes as NA.

set.seed(100)
N = 500L
period = 5L
n = N*period
id = rep(1:N, each = period)
u = rnorm(N)
v = -0.5*u + sqrt(0.75)*rnorm(N)
eps = rnorm(n)
e = 0.5*eps + sqrt(0.75)*rnorm(n)
w = rnorm(n)
x = rnorm(n)
d = as.integer(x + w + u[id] + eps > 0)
y = x + w + v[id] + e
y[d == 0] = NA
dat = data.frame(id = id, time = rep(seq_len(period), N), x = x, w = w,
                 d = d, y = y)

3.2. Fit the model and compare estimates

Fit the equations jointly using 10-node GH integration.

fit = panel_select(
  outcome = y ~ x + w,
  selection = d ~ x + w,
  data = dat,
  id = "id",
  family = "linear",
  control = panel_control(quadrature = "gh", nodes = 10L))

Table 2 reports precomputed estimates from GH10, AGH10 and GL10 alongside the known parameters. The AGH and GL fits are not rerun when this vignette is built. For AGH or GL, keep nodes = 10L and change quadrature to "agh" or "gl", respectively. Standard errors use the default OPG covariance.

3.3. Check and refine the fit

Step 1. Check convergence and stationarity. The optimizer should report convergence, and Q_OPG should be close to zero. The package warns when Q_OPG > 0.1 by default.

check_stationarity(fit)[c("optimizer_converged", "Q_OPG")]
#> $optimizer_converged
#> [1] TRUE
#> 
#> $Q_OPG
#> [1] 5.087207e-07

A small Q_OPG indicates that the fitted numerical objective is close to stationary. Integration accuracy requires a separate check.

Step 2. Compare calculations with more nodes. Re-evaluate the 10-node fit using 20 nodes while holding the parameter estimates fixed:

check_quadrature(fit, nodes = 20L)
#> Quadrature check (higher_order) of: Fixed Gauss-Hermite (gh), 10 x 10 nodes (selection x outcome)
#>           rule nodes loglik_change max_panel_change max_score_change
#>  person_effect    20       0.00131          0.00756           0.0329
#>  max_panel_score_change within_tolerance
#>                    0.06            FALSE
#> Tolerances: loglik 1e-05, score 1e-04; optimizer converged; covariance valid

Although the differences between the 10-node and 20-node GH calculations exceed the default comparison tolerances, refitting with 20 nodes changes the parameter estimates only slightly. To refine the fit, refit with 20 nodes and repeat the check using 30 nodes. If notable sensitivity persists, consider AGH or GL. Because GL applies the specified number of nodes within each integration segment, GL10 generally requires substantially more computation than GH10 or AGH10. GL10 or GL12 may be sufficient for many applications.

Step 3. Refit with more nodes if needed. Use update() to fit at the higher order:

fit_more = update(fit, control = modifyList(fit$control, list(nodes = 20L)))
coef(fit_more) - coef(fit)

update() uses the fitted coefficients as starting values and preserves the other controls. Compare the estimates, then repeat the checks on fit_more with a higher comparison order.

4. Practical Modeling Considerations

This section highlights several practical considerations while using this package.

  • As shown in Bailey and Peng (2025), panel selection models typically achieve stronger identification than their cross-sectional counterparts.

  • The panel need not be balanced. Also, individuals are allowed to make different participation decisions at different time periods.

  • The covariates used in the first-stage and second-stage equations may differ, although excluding a variable from one stage typically requires theoretical justification.

  • The model allows the two correlation parameters \(\rho\) and \(\tau\) to be turned off separately or jointly. In panel_select(), use fixed = c(cor_re = 0) to set \(\rho=0\), fixed = c(cor_error = 0) to set \(\tau=0\), or fixed = c(cor_re = 0, cor_error = 0) to set both to zero. Fixing one correlation at zero can substantially speed up estimation, especially when \(\tau=0\).

  • When the outcome variable is observed regardless of participation, the endogeneity package may be more appropriate, although it currently focuses on cross-sectional settings.

For further details, see the help pages for model fitting, numerical controls, and prediction.
